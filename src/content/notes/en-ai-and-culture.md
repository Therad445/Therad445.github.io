One question keeps coming back to me: what happens to writing when producing it becomes much easier than reading it?

Generative AI has many useful applications. Here, though, I’m interested in something else: we can now quickly produce coherent explanations, arguments and entire stories. A reader still has roughly the same amount of time and attention. Being able to prepare ten pieces in an evening does not mean anyone needs all ten.

In my slides on AI and culture, I called this an “inflation of meaning”. That is probably too broad. What I mean is an inflation of the outward signs of thoughtful writing. Smooth transitions, a confident tone and careful formatting can all be present in a text that gives us very little to think about. Meaning does not disappear. It becomes harder to distinguish from a convincing imitation.

## Who is speaking?

I want to know where a claim comes from. Has the writer tested something, read the source or built it themselves? Or are they retelling someone else’s retelling? The difference is especially visible in technical writing. “It became faster” tells us little without the measurement conditions. A table of unsuccessful runs can sometimes explain the work better than a page of confident conclusions.

With literature, it is more complicated. I don’t expect an experimental protocol from a story. But I’m interested in why the writer chose that particular story, left an awkward sentence in place, or decided not to explain something. Those details make it feel like a conversation with a particular person. Constantly adjusting a text to the reader’s presumed expectations risks losing the very thing that made it worth reading.

This is not a simple test for detecting machine-written text. People write formulaically too, and a model can help someone produce good work. What matters more to me is what the author is willing to stand behind, and what they are willing to reconsider.

## What to do with the time we save

I can imagine two uses for the same technology. We could produce more material, take up more space in a feed and respond faster to any topic. Or we could spend the time we save reading sources, checking a calculation or trying again to understand an unsuccessful experiment.

I prefer the second possibility. It leaves room for work that is not always visible in the result. A finished article rarely tells you how many ideas were discarded. A good photograph does not show all the shots that failed. Being able to obtain a first draft quickly does not remove the need to choose.

That is why I’m interested in what happens to craft. If a tool can take over some routine work, will we have more patience for difficult things? Or will we get used to expecting every answer immediately? I don’t have a confident prediction. Both habits can coexist, even in the same person.

For this website, I want to keep a simple rule: publish things to which I have something of my own to add. Sometimes that will be working code, sometimes an account of a mistake, sometimes a thought about a book. There may not be many posts. I would like the person still trying to understand things to remain visible behind them.
